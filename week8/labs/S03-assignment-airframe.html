Brief DRooftop Equipment LiftFacilities · repeated heavy lift

Operator Facilities engineering, a hospital campus

The chiller plant on the roof of the main ward block sits 60 m above the street and is serviced four times a year. Everything it needs — compressor parts, filter banks, tooling, drums of glycol — currently goes up in the building's service lift, which then belongs to the works and not to the hospital for the length of the job. The alternative is a mobile crane, which closes the street below for a full day, requires a road-closure permit, and costs more than the parts being lifted.

Facilities engineering want to fly the loads up instead: off a marked square of car park, over the corner of the building, onto a pad on the roof, and back. They are not interested in a demonstration. They want to know whether an aircraft can do this twelve times in a working day, for years, and — because the route crosses a public street — what happens to it when something breaks.

What you must deliver

Repeated vertical delivery of a slung or racked load from a ground staging area to a rooftop pad, with a stationary hold over the pad long enough for the load to be released by hand.

The numbers

Cargo mass
4.0 kg per lift, carried beneath the aircraft
Lift height
60 m from the staging area to the rooftop pad
Horizontal distance
400 m from the staging square to the building
Lifts per working day
12, with the aircraft returning empty between them
Hold over the pad
≥ 30 s stationary, while the load is unhooked
Position hold while releasing
Within ± 0.5 m, with a person beside the pad

Where you will be operating

  • The route crosses a public street. It is closed to pedestrians for the few seconds of each transit, but it cannot be closed for the day, and the operator will not accept an aircraft that falls out of the sky because one motor stopped.
  • The gap between the ward block and the building opposite is an urban canyon. Wind accelerates through it and gusts reach 10 m/s at rooftop height on days that are calm at street level.
  • 35–42 °C through the working day, with the roof itself considerably hotter than the air above the car park
  • The staging area is a marked square of car park, 8 × 8 m, with parked vehicles on three sides. There is no hangar, no power beyond what the van carries, and no shelter.

What the aircraft must carry

  • The 4.0 kg cargo itself, which draws no power
  • A powered release mechanism, so nobody has to reach under a running aircraft. Allow 250 g and 5 W for the mechanism and its controller.
  • The cargo is slung or racked beneath the aircraft. It is not aerodynamic, it is not always the same shape, and on a windy day it will swing.

What you cannot change

  • Total system cost ≤ $16,000
  • A two-person crew: one flying, one on the roof receiving the load. Neither is free to do anything else during a lift.
  • The whole system travels in the back of a van, in a load bay 1.2 × 1.0 m, together with the crew and the cargo for the day. If it does not fit in that bay it does not reach the site.
  • A single motor failure must not result in the loss of the aircraft. Facilities engineering asked this question in writing, and they will read the answer.

Yours to decide, and to defend

Rotor count and layout. Frame size. Thrust-to-weight, and whether you computed it with the cargo aboard or without it. How many aircraft do the twelve lifts. Battery chemistry, pack count and swap strategy. What the aircraft does over the street when a motor stops.

Two of this brief's own constraints pull against each other, and the numbers say by how much. Find them, name them in your report, and say what you gave up. A team that reports no tension here has not measured anything.

Brief ESolar Farm Thermal SurveyAsset performance · area survey

Operator Asset performance, a utility-scale photovoltaic plant

A photovoltaic string that has failed looks exactly like one that is working. The panels are identical from the ground, the inverter reports the string and not the panel, and the only cheap way to find a bad cell is that it runs hot — a failed bypass diode or a cracked cell shows up in thermal imagery as a bright rectangle among thousands of dark ones.

The plant is surveyed by an external contractor twice a year. In between, a string that fails in the first week of a period is generating nothing for up to six months before anyone notices. Asset performance want to own the capability and fly it monthly, on the same lines every time, so that the same panel can be compared against itself.

What you must deliver

A complete thermal and visual survey of the array, flown on repeatable lines, at a resolution fine enough that a single warm cell can be identified and located.

The numbers

Array area
30 hectares — 300,000 m² of panels and access tracks
Thermal resolution
≤ 8 cm per pixel across the whole array
Visual resolution
≤ 2 cm per pixel, flown at the same time
Image overlap
70% forward, 60% side
Survey window
09:00 to 12:00 — the panels must be hot and the sun high
Battery sets available
3, and charging is slower than flying

Where you will be operating

  • Open, flat and utterly featureless. No obstructions above panel height, no shade anywhere on the site, and nothing to hide behind if the weather turns.
  • 38–45 °C, rising through the survey window. The array itself radiates, so the air above it is hotter than the forecast.
  • Wind 6–11 m/s, strengthening noticeably after 11:00, with blowing dust. The back half of the window is the windy half.
  • The site is a 40-minute drive from anywhere. Whatever you did not bring, you do not have; whatever breaks, stays broken until next month.

What the aircraft must carry

  • A radiometric thermal camera — 640 × 512 pixels, 12 μm pitch, 13 mm lens — and a visual camera, 5472 pixels across, 4 μm pitch, 24 mm lens, on one down-looking mount
  • Allow 450 g and 9 W for both sensors and the mount together
  • The thermal sensor integrates for 16 ms. Smear beyond one thermal pixel makes a warm cell unreadable, so the resolution you must achieve limits how fast you may fly as well as how high.
  • Imagery is recorded onboard. No live downlink is required.

What you cannot change

  • Total system cost ≤ $11,000
  • One operator, who also drives, unloads, sets up and packs down alone
  • Charging is from a vehicle inverter only — assume you cannot charge faster than you fly, so the three battery sets are the whole energy budget for the morning
  • Must operate at 45 °C without thermal shutdown, on a site with no shade to cool anything in

Yours to decide, and to defend

Survey altitude and ground speed — both follow from numbers already in this brief, and neither is a free choice. Rotor count, propeller diameter and frame size. How many sorties the three battery sets buy you, and whether that covers 30 hectares. Battery chemistry, and what you checked before choosing it.

Brief FBridge and Culvert InspectionStructures · confined-space inspection

Operator Regional roads authority, structures team

Bridge undersides and drainage culverts are inspected on a five-year cycle by a team with a boat, a ladder and a torch. The undersides are awkward and slow. The culverts are worse: anything a person walks into counts as a confined-space entry, which means a permit, a gas test, a standby team at the mouth and a rescue plan before the first person goes in — half a day of preparation for twenty minutes of looking at concrete.

The structures team want imagery good enough for a condition assessment — cracking, spalling, exposed reinforcement, joint condition — from an aircraft, with nobody entering anything. They are entirely realistic about what that means. They expect the aircraft to be flown into things.

What you must deliver

Close-range imagery of bridge undersides and culvert interiors, lit by the aircraft, positioned well enough that a defect can be found again on the next visit.

The numbers

Structures per campaign
14 bridges and culverts on one road network
Standoff from the surface
1.5 m — closer risks contact, further loses detail
Smallest culvert
2.5 m wide × 3.0 m high, and 40 m long end to end
Position hold while capturing
Within ± 0.3 m, with no GPS
Useful time inside per sortie
≥ 18 minutes, not counting getting in, getting out or reserve
Lighting
Continuous, from the aircraft — there is none in there

Where you will be operating

  • GPS is unusable under a bridge deck and absent inside a culvert. Whatever holds the aircraft still, it is not satellite navigation.
  • There is no ambient light at all inside a culvert. Forty metres in, the aircraft is the only light source, and the lighting runs for the whole sortie rather than in flashes.
  • Wind funnels hard at culvert mouths and under decks, then stops dead a few metres inside. The transition is abrupt and it happens in the least forgiving place on the route.
  • Surfaces are wet concrete and steel, frequently within a rotor's reach on more than one side at once.
  • Contact with the structure is expected. Not a hazard to be designed against — an ordinary event, several times per campaign, after which the aircraft is expected to keep flying.

What the aircraft must carry

  • A visual camera on a mount that can look upward as well as forward, plus LED lighting
  • Allow 350 g and 15 W for camera, mount and lighting together
  • The lighting is most of that power and it never switches off. On an aircraft this size it is not a rounding error in the energy budget.

What you cannot change

  • Total system cost ≤ $7,000 — the lowest budget of the three briefs, for the fiddliest job
  • A single operator on foot, carrying the aircraft, the batteries and the ground station to each structure, sometimes down an embankment
  • The aircraft must survive contact with concrete and keep flying, repeatedly, without a rebuild between structures
  • It must physically fit and manoeuvre inside a 2.5 m culvert — fitting through the opening is not the same as being able to fly in there

Yours to decide, and to defend

Frame size first, and everything else after it. Rotor count and layout. What form the propeller protection takes, and what it costs you in mass and in efficiency. How you hold position without GPS. Whether one aircraft covers all 14 structures, and how many battery sets one person can carry.

01 The deciding parameter Core

One sentence. Each brief has a different parameter doing the deciding — find yours before you choose anything.

02 Rotor count Core

Pick one, then say what you rejected and why. More rotors is not free — it buys redundancy and costs mass, cost and interference.

Given — the motor-out thrust floor

To keep flying after losing one motor of n, the surviving motors must be able to produce T/W ≥ n/(n−1) between them:
Quadn/(n−1) = 4/3 = 1.33
Hex6/5 = 1.20
Octo8/7 = 1.14
A hexacopter with no thrust margin does not survive a motor failure. Rotor count alone decides nothing.

03 Arm layout Core

Layout follows what you carry and where it must see, far more often than flight performance.

04 Thrust-to-weight target Core

Justify it from your brief's environment — wind, payload, the manoeuvring the task needs. Not from the slide.

05 Size Core

A propeller diameter and a frame span that fit your mission and your site. The frame span is the input to S04's structure mass, so make it a real number.

06 The failure case Core

Name the failure your problem statement requires you to address, then say honestly whether your airframe survives it.
