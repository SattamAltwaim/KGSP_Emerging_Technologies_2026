Session S02

Requirements, screening and the matrix

Everything you need for the exercise, on one page. There are no answers here — the answers depend on your brief, and working them out is the exercise. Your brief is inside the sheets.

01 What a requirement is

subject  +  shall  +  a quantity with units  +  a way to verify it

If you cannot say how you would test it, it is not a requirement yet. The verification method is the part everyone forgets.

Not a requirement

  • "Good battery life."
  • "Use a six-motor multirotor." — a solution smuggled in
  • "Fly the survey quickly."

A requirement

  • "Complete the survey and land with ≥ 20% battery, verified from the flight log."
  • "Remain controllable after any single motor failure, verified by a bench cut test."
  • "Cover 500 × 400 m at ≤ 3 cm/pixel in one sortie, verified by GSD measured on a ground target."
Write three. Only three. They become the three rows of your matrix, so a requirement you would not score is a requirement you should not write. Then rank them — the ranking becomes your weights.

02 Requirement or constraint?

RequirementConstraint
It isSomething your design must achieveSomething your design must live inside
Where fromYou chose it, from the missionYou did not choose it and cannot argue with it
What it doesRequirements score. Rows of your matrixConstraints filter. They throw options out before scoring
Example"Shall cover the survey area on one battery""Budget ≤ $8,000" · "45 °C without shutdown"

Seven places a constraint hides

Budget (system cost, not airframe cost) · Payload (mass and power — both are given) · Crew (how many, and what else they are doing) · Launch & recovery — read this row twice, it rules out more than any other · Environment (wind, temperature, ground) · Schedule (time available) · Transport (how it reaches site, and who carries it).

03 Turning a number in the brief into a requirement

Ground sample distance — what the camera can resolve
GSD = p · H ⁄ f   ⟹   H = GSD · f ⁄ p
GSD
Ground distance one pixel covers — the number in your brief
p
Pixel pitch on the sensor, typically 2–4 μm
f
Lens focal length, mm
H
Height above the ground — the answer, not a choice

A resolution requirement is an altitude constraint in disguise. Once the brief states a GSD, your flying height is decided for you.

Coverage rate — what one aircraft can actually sweep
Ȧ = W (1 − s) V      t = Atotal ⁄ Ȧ
Ȧ
Coverage rate — ground area swept per second, m²/s
W
Swath the camera sees across track. W = GSD × pixels across
s
Side overlap. 60% overlap means you bank only 40% of each pass
V
Ground speed — yours to choose, the only free variable
t
Flying time it demands — the answer

Divide that flying time by a plausible endurance and you have the number of sorties — which is very often the thing that decides the configuration.

04 The method: screen, then score

1
Screen against your constraints. Binary — pass or fail. An option that violates a constraint is out; it does not get a score, it gets one line naming the constraint that killed it. Give all four classes a verdict, including the ones you already know are wrong: a blank row reads as a class you never considered.
2
Score the survivors against your requirements. Weighted. Everything that got through screening is genuinely viable, so now you are asking which is best, not which is possible.
A weak fit is a low score, not an OUT. "The wing is wasted on this mission" is a scoring argument. Only screen something out when a line of the brief makes it impossible.

The four classes

ClassWhat it gives youWhat it costs
MultirotorHovers, holds a point, lands on anything flatEverything it does, it does on hover power

Filling the matrix

  • Criteria are your three requirements, one per row.
  • Weights come from your own ranking — most important becomes 5.
  • Scores are 1–5 against the criterion, judged by you and defended by you.
  • Total only after every score is in. A margin of one or two points is not a decision, it is a tie — when that happens, find the criterion you left out or challenge a weight, and say which you did.

05 Three ways teams lose marks

Decided first, scored backwards

  • Everyone does it and nobody notices.
  • Fix: fill every score before you total anything. Have one person score a column without seeing the others.

Every weight is a 3

  • If it all matters equally, the matrix is doing no work.
  • Fix: you ranked your three requirements — use the ranking.

No record of what you rejected

  • The report shows one option and one winner, which reads as a decision made before the exercise started.
  • Fix: the rejections and their reasons are marked as heavily as the choice. Write them down as you go.

06 Before you hand it in

Run this test on your own list. If your three requirements would work just as well for somebody else's brief, they are too vague to be requirements. Three teams, three environments, three sets of numbers — your list should be unusable by anyone else in this room.

You will be asked these four at the review

  1. Your mission in one sentence, and what you chose
  2. The option that was hardest to reject, and the constraint that killed it
  3. The weight your answer is most sensitive to
  4. What evidence would change your mind

Question 4 is the one that matters. An engineer who cannot say what would change their mind has not made a decision — they have expressed a preference.
