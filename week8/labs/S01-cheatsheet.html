Session S01

Choosing a configuration for a mission

Everything from this session that the four-mission exercise actually uses. There are no answers here — the reasoning is what is being asked for, and one of the four missions has no clean answer at all. Nothing in this session is graded.

01 The four configuration classes

ClassHow it holds itself upWhat it gives youWhat it costs
MultirotorFour or more fixed-pitch rotors, thrust set by rpmHovers, holds a point, lands on anything flatEverything it does, it does on hover power
Fixed-wingA wing, in forward flight onlyRange and endurance nothing else comes nearCannot stop. Needs a way up and a way down
HelicopterOne large rotor, variable pitchBest hover of any rotorcraft, and it cruises tooMechanically complex, costly, maintained constantly
VTOL hybridA wing plus lift rotors or tiltsWing range with vertical launch and recoveryYou carry both systems and tune two flight regimes
Four classes, not four products. They are not four different kinds of physics — they are the same airfoil used two different ways. A wing is one flown forward; a rotor is one spun in a circle; a VTOL carries both.

02 The two facts every argument comes back to

Hovering is expensive

A rotor holds you up by throwing air downwards, continuously, for as long as you are up there.

Phover ∝ W3⁄2 ⁄ √A
P
Power to hover
W
Weight
A
Total disc area

Heavier costs more than proportionally. Bigger discs cost less — double the disc area and you save about 30% of the hover power at the same weight.

A wing is not

A wing holds you up by moving forward. You stop paying for weight and start paying only for drag.

L = ½ ρ V2 S CL    T = W ⁄ (L/D)
L
Lift
ρ
Air density
V
Airspeed
S
Wing area
CL
Lift coefficient — set by angle of attack
L/D
Lift-to-drag ratio

A lift-to-drag ratio of 12 means one twelfth of your weight in thrust keeps you up. A rotor gets no such discount.

A wing covers far more ground per joule; a rotor can stop. Every configuration argument is those two facts in tension.

03 The three questions that decide a configuration

1
Where does it launch from, and what catches it coming back? A runway, a catapult, somebody's arm. Every one of those is a site constraint — and it usually decides the aircraft before any performance number is considered.
2
Does the job need the aircraft to stop? Inspection and search do. Delivery and wide-area survey do not. That single question splits the configurations cleanly in half, and it is answered by the mission, never by the engineer.
3
What happens if you lose it? An airframe you accept losing stops needing a recovery system at all. An airframe you cannot afford to lose buys redundancy with mass.
Not one of those three is about top speed, endurance or payload. Those matter — but they come later, and you cannot size an aircraft until you know what kind of aircraft it is.

04 What to write for each mission

  • The configuration class you would fly.
  • The single constraint that decided it — one sentence, no more. If you need three sentences, you have not found the constraint yet.
  • What you rejected, and what killed it. Where an obvious alternative exists, say why it lost.
One of the four is genuinely arguable. Find it, and say what extra information would settle it. For that one, ask yourself whether two aircraft is a better answer than any single aircraft.

05 You will be asked these four

  1. Which class you picked
  2. The one constraint that decided it
  3. What you rejected, and what killed it
  4. What would change your mind

Question 4 is the one that matters. An engineer who cannot say what would change their mind has not made a decision — they have expressed a preference.
