Session S03

Rotor count, thrust margin and size

Everything you need for the exercise, on one page. There are no answers here — the answers depend on your brief, and working them out is the exercise. Your brief is inside the sheet.

01 What a multirotor can actually control

It cannot change the pitch of its blades. The only thing it can change is how fast each motor spins — and out of that it has to produce four independent controls.

ControlHow it is produced
HeightSpeed all of them up together
Roll and pitchSpeed up one side, slow the other. The aircraft tilts, and the tilted thrust pushes it sideways — a multirotor moves by leaning
YawEvery spinning propeller drags the airframe the opposite way. Half spin each way and it cancels — until you speed one diagonal pair up
Four things to control, and each motor is one knob. That single sentence decides more of this session than any equation on it. Count your knobs before you count your motors.

02 Rotor count — what more of them buys and costs

LayoutRotorsBuys youCosts you
Quadcopter4Simplest, lightest, cheapest, most efficient for its sizeDoes not survive a motor failure — three knobs cannot answer four demands
Hexacopter6Survives one motor out, given enough thrust marginMore mass, more cost, six ESCs and six arms
Octocopter8Most tolerant. The layout for flying over what must not be hitHeaviest and dearest, and large for its disc area

03 Thrust-to-weight, and what a lean costs

Thrust margin
T ⁄ W = n Tmotor ⁄ mg      Tneeded = mg ⁄ cos φ
T/W
Thrust-to-weight ratio, dimensionless. Each motor makes (T/W)·mg/n
n
Rotor count
m, g
Takeoff mass in kg; 9.81 m/s²
φ
Lean angle. At 30°, you need 15% more thrust to hold height
RatioWhat it is for
1.5 : 1Barely flyable. No reserve for gusts, no authority to correct an upset. Not a design point
2 : 1The normal target. Half holds you up, half is available to manoeuvre and fight wind
2.5 : 1Windy sites, precise position holding, recovery near a structure
3 : 1 +Aerobatic and racing. You are carrying motor mass a survey will never use
Wind is paid for out of your thrust margin. A rotor tilted into a gust puts less of its thrust into holding you up. And if your aircraft carries a payload, work the ratio out with the payload aboard — an empty aircraft is a different aircraft.

04 Surviving a motor failure

The thrust floor — where it comes from
T ⁄ W ≥ n ⁄ (n − 1)
Why
Each motor makes (T/W)·W/n. Lose one and n−1 are left; together they must still hold up W
RotorsFloorWhat actually happens
41.33Does not survive, and not for want of thrust. Three knobs, four demands — the autopilot abandons yaw and the aircraft spins
61.20Survives, given sufficient margin. Five left, each +20%
81.14Survives with margin to spare. Seven left, each +14%
"Redundant" is not a property of the rotor count alone. It is a property of the rotor count and the thrust margin together. Six motors at 90% of hover thrust is T/W = 1.11, under the 1.20 floor, and it comes down exactly like a quadcopter.

05 What it costs to hold an aircraft up

Hover power — momentum theory
Pideal = T3⁄2 ⁄ √(2ρA)      P = Pideal ⁄ (FM · η)
P
Hover power, W
T
Thrust, N. In hover T = mg
A
Disc area — every rotor added together, m²
ρ
Air density, 1.225 kg/m³
FM, η
Figure of merit 0.65; motor and ESC efficiency 0.85

Power rises with weight to the power 1.5 — add 20% of mass and you pay about 33% more power. That is why mass is the thing S04 chases, and why adding a gram is never free.

Disc loading — the same fact, named
DL = W ⁄ A  [N/m²]   ⟹   P ⁄ W = (1 ⁄ FM) √(DL ⁄ 2ρ)
DL
Weight carried per m² of disc
P/W
Power per newton of aircraft. Lower is better
Halve the disc loading and you save about 30% of your hover power, at exactly the same takeoff weight. Nothing else available to you is worth that much — which is why a survey multirotor looks nothing like a racing one.

06 Propellers — why you cannot just fit bigger ones

Scaling laws
T = CT ρ n² D⁴      P = CP ρ n³ D⁵
n
Revolutions per second — not per minute
D
Propeller diameter, m
CT
Thrust coefficient. Each blade is a wing going round at local airspeed Ω·r
  • Diameter wins whenever you have room. D⁴ against n², so growing the disc beats spinning it faster.
  • Tip speed is π·D·n. Past 200 m/s the tips go transonic, so a big propeller must turn slowly. This is the one hard physical limit in the session.
  • Bigger propellers also mean a bigger frame, a heavier motor, slower response to gusts — and they may simply not fit your site.

07 Arm layout

LayoutWhat it isWhen
XArms at the corners. Nothing ahead of the camera, every motor works roll and pitchThe default. Choose it deliberately, not by omission
+One arm forward, so a propeller sits in the shotRare on anything carrying a camera
HTwin booms, a clear bay down the centre. Heavier for the same spanWhen the payload is long, or needs the centre clear
Layout follows what you carry and where it must see, far more often than it follows flight performance. Work out what your sensor must not have in front of it, and the layout usually chooses itself.

08 Before you hand it in

Run this test on your own answer. Every number on your sheet — the rotor count, the thrust-to-weight target, the propeller diameter, the frame span — should be traceable to a sentence in your brief. If a number came from this page instead, it is not yet an answer.

Three teams, three briefs, and there is no reason at all for you to arrive at the same airframe. If two of you did, one of you has copied a default rather than read a brief.

You will be asked these four at the review

  1. The one parameter that decided your airframe
  2. Your rotor count and layout, and what you rejected
  3. Your thrust-to-weight target, and where it came from
  4. What happens to your aircraft when one motor stops

Question 4 is the one that gets pressed. "It's a hexacopter, so it's fine" is not an answer — the follow-up is what is your thrust margin, and the floor for six rotors is 1.20.
